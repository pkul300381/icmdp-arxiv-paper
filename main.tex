\begin{abstract}
Natural-language interfaces allow users to impose explicit restrictions on
agents acting in sequential environments. Reward maximization alone does not
distinguish an action that is prohibited from one that is merely undesirable.
We formulate an \emph{Intent-Constrained Markov Decision Process} (ICMDP), in
which a compiled intent rule is intersected with baseline operational
availability to define the effective admissible set.

The principal contribution is a validated, versioned language-to-admissibility
architecture. An LLM proposes a typed structured specification; deterministic
validation and compilation produce a reusable state-evaluable rule; and the
same rule restricts hypothetical actions during planning and filters proposals
at execution. The LLM remains outside the fast decision loop and the trusted
enforcement boundary. Atomic version activation separates enforcement of a
validated rule from subsequent policy optimization. Subject to correct specification, state
information, activation, and filtering, the executed policy assigns zero
probability to actions excluded by the active rule. We state feasibility
conditions, show that standard discounted dynamic-programming guarantees carry
over under fixed admissibility, relate statewise exclusions to a zero-budget
CMDP encoding, and show how standard restricted-action methods apply. The
results are theoretical and are illustrated through an autonomous
cloud-operations example.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Intent-Constrained MDP (ICMDP), Markov Decision
Processes, Constrained MDPs, Intent Constraints, Large Language Models,
AI Alignment, Safe Reinforcement Learning.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) provide the standard model of sequential
decision-making under uncertainty \citep{puterman1994}. Deployed agents also
face partial observability, operational constraints, and instructions supplied
through conversational interfaces. This paper focuses on the last of these:
the translation of user-specified hard restrictions into an action-selection
mechanism. Partial observability and cumulative resource constraints remain
relevant boundaries, but are not the object addressed here.

A finite negative reward changes the relative desirability of an action
but does not remove that action from the feasible set; if its downstream
reward is sufficiently large, the action may still be optimal. This differs
from an instruction intended to make the action inadmissible.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to mediate between users
and decision-making systems, making language-specified hard restrictions
especially relevant. Consider an autonomous cloud-operations agent managing a
production service. Its global action vocabulary may contain deployment,
rollback, restart, scaling, and no-operation actions. The instruction
\emph{``Do not deploy while a Severity-1 production incident is active''} must be
translated into a formal rule enforced during action selection. Reward
learning does not itself provide this enforcement interface, while expected
cumulative constraints require a separate encoding of the instruction as costs
and thresholds.
The deployment action remains operationally meaningful and may be
available during normal operation, yet the fixed rule excludes it in a state
with an active Severity-1 (Sev1) incident. This is precisely the setting for a
state-dependent admissible set: the action belongs to the global vocabulary,
but its admissibility depends on the current state. The instruction has not
changed merely because the same rule returns different sets in normal and
Sev1 states.

The cloud-operations scenario is used throughout only to make the
general framework concrete. The same lifecycle can apply to autonomous
purchasing, trading, tool-use agents, and other sequential decision systems.

Accordingly, the paper presents a framework for converting language into
admissibility rules and enforcement mechanisms rather than claiming a new
class of Bellman equations. Its central object is the lifecycle by which a
user's linguistic instruction becomes a validated, executable predicate used
both during planning and at the final action-selection boundary.

The paper's central contribution is a validated and versioned
language-to-admissibility lifecycle: natural language is mapped to a validated
typed specification, a deterministic compiler produces one executable rule,
and that same rule is visible both to prospective planning over hypothetical
states and to the final runtime gate. The LLM remains outside the trusted
boundary, while atomic version activation separates enforcement of a validated
instruction from re-optimization of behavior. Three supporting
analyses establish the formal and algorithmic setting:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(\arabic*)]
  \item the ICMDP interface, its feasibility conditions, and the standard
  discounted dynamic-programming properties inherited under fixed
  admissibility (Section~\ref{sec:icmdp});
  \item the precise relationship between statewise intent exclusions and a
  zero-budget CMDP encoding, together with a versioned protocol for changing
  intent (Sections~\ref{subsec:optimality} and~\ref{subsec:extensions}); and
  \item compatibility with standard restricted-action planning and model-free
  algorithms, including their computational costs and failure conditions
  (Section~\ref{sec:algorithms}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{subsec:related}

\paragraph{Constrained and safe decision-making.}
The theoretical foundations of MDPs and stochastic dynamic programming are
due to \citet{puterman1994}, with the connection to modern reinforcement
learning (RL) algorithms established in \citet{sutton2018}. CMDPs extend MDPs
by constraining expected cumulative costs \citep{altman1999}.
\citet{achiam2017cpo} proposed Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO), a
policy-search algorithm with guarantees for near-constraint satisfaction at
each iteration; \citet{garcia2015} survey safe RL more broadly.
\citet{roy2022behavior} use CMDP constraints as a direct interface for
specifying behavior that would otherwise be expressed through reward
engineering. These approaches retain expected cumulative cost as the primary
constraint representation.

\paragraph{Action masking, shielding, and state-dependent constraints.}
A separate line of work restricts actions directly. Invalid-action masking
removes unavailable actions before sampling or policy evaluation
\citep{huang2022}, while action-constrained RL treats statewise feasibility as
part of policy optimization \citep{lin2021actionconstrained}. Shielding
synthesizes an action-level intervention mechanism from temporal-logic
specifications \citep{alshiekh2018shielding}. Specification-guided RL likewise
studies formal specifications as an alternative to reward-only task definition
\citep{jothimurugan2025specification}. These methods establish the importance
of planner- or learner-visible constraints and runtime intervention, but begin
from formal or manually supplied specifications rather than a validated
natural-language compilation pipeline.

\paragraph{Language-derived objectives and constraints.}
Preference learning and RLHF typically encode qualitative behavior in a
learned reward model \citep{christiano2017,leike2018}. More closely related
work translates language into explicit decision artifacts.
\citet{chua2025nlconstraints} infer reward and constraint functions and place
them in a CMDP for language agents. \citet{jegal2025alamp} use a staged LLM
pipeline to generate an MDP environment and trained policy from a free-form
task description. \citet{winston2026solver} translate natural-language
tool-use policies into SMT constraints and intercept proposed tool calls at
runtime. The last system is particularly close to the enforcement side of the
present architecture; its formal checks apply at the tool-call boundary,
whereas ICMDP also exposes the same compiled rule to MDP planning over
hypothetical successor states. In adjacent application-management work,
\citet{dzeparoska2024policy} use LLMs to decompose intents into executable
policies and closed management loops. That systems pipeline addresses intent
realization, whereas ICMDP centers planner-visible statewise admissibility and
a separate hard runtime gate.

\paragraph{Positioning of this work.}
State-dependent action sets, constrained optimization, masking, and shielding
are established mechanisms. ICMDP does not claim novelty for the restricted
Bellman operator. Its contribution is the connection of a learned semantic
front end to a deterministic, versioned specification boundary: one validated
rule governs both planning over future states and filtering at execution, with
explicit failure handling, atomic updates, and audit records. We position this
combination as the distinguishing feature of the framework relative to reward-
or cost-only language grounding, language-to-MDP generation, and runtime-only
policy checking. Example~\ref{ex:runtime_only} illustrates why planner-visible
admissibility is not operationally equivalent to applying the same rule only
as a final runtime gate.


\subsection{Preliminaries}
\label{subsec:prelim}

\begin{definition}[MDP]
A \emph{Markov Decision Process} is a tuple
$\cM = (\cS, \cA, \cP, R, \gamma)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\cS$ is a finite set of states,
  \item $\cA$ is a finite set of actions,
  \item $\cP : \cS \times \cA \times \cS \to [0,1]$ is the transition
  probability function, with $\cP(s' \given s, a)$ denoting the probability
  of transitioning to state $s'$ upon taking action $a$ in state $s$,
  \item $R : \cS \times \cA \to \R$ is the reward function. Here
  $R(s,a)$ denotes the expected one-step reward
  associated with the state--action pair $(s,a)$.
  \item $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Let $\rho\in\Delta(\cS)$ denote the initial-state distribution. Expectations
over trajectories generated from $\rho$ under policy $\pi$ are written
$\E_{\rho,\pi}$. These expectations average over the initial-state
distribution, policy randomization when present, and stochastic state
transitions.

A \emph{stationary policy} $\pi : \cS \to \Delta(\cA)$ specifies the
probability $\pi(a \given s)$ of selecting action $a$ in state $s$, where
$\Delta(\cA)$ denotes the probability simplex over $\cA$.
Thus $\pi(\cdot\given s)$ is the agent's action-selection distribution
in state $s$, whereas $\cP(\cdot\given s,a)$ is the environment's next-state
distribution after action $a$ is selected.

The \emph{optimal value function} satisfies the \emph{Bellman optimality
equation}:
\begin{equation}
  V^*(s) = \max_{a \in \cA}\!\left[R(s,a) + \gamma
    \sum_{s' \in \cS} \cP(s' \given s, a)\, V^*(s')\right].
  \label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
Any maximizing action induces an optimal deterministic policy.

This MDP formulation assumes sufficient state observability, imposes no
operational constraints, and encodes the entire objective within the scalar
reward $R$. POMDPs replace direct state access with a belief distribution
\citep{kaelbling1998}. Belief-dependent or robust admissibility under partial
observability is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\emph{Constrained MDPs} add expected-cost constraints to this model.

\begin{definition}[CMDP \citep{altman1999}]
A \emph{Constrained Markov Decision Process} augments an MDP with a set of
cost functions and associated thresholds:
$\cM_C = (\cS, \cA, \cP, R, \{C_k\}_{k=1}^n, \{d_k\}_{k=1}^n, \gamma)$,
where $C_k : \cS \times \cA \to \R$ is the $k$-th cost function and
$d_k \in \R$ is the permitted upper bound, or
budget, on the expected discounted cumulative cost associated with $C_k$.
\end{definition}

The CMDP optimization problem is:
\begin{equation}
  \max_{\pi}\; \E_{\rho,\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t)\right]
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad
  \E_{\rho,\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t C_k(s_t, a_t)\right] \leq d_k,
  \quad k = 1,\ldots,n.
  \label{eq:cmdp}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cmdp} constrains expected discounted cumulative
cost. For a positive budget $d_k>0$, feasibility does not in general imply
that every realized trajectory is violation-free.
For a nonnegative violation cost, zero budget can encode zero discounted
violation occupancy along trajectories induced by the specified initial-state
distribution and policy; Proposition~\ref{prop:cmdp_encoding} makes the
relationship precise. ICMDP instead exposes the statewise admissible-action set
as an interface for compilation, planning, filtering, and audit.

The distinction between cumulative and statewise constraints also
depends on the state representation. For example, a
pathwise cumulative resource
limit can be made state-local by augmenting the state with the remaining
budget $b_t$; an action of cost $c(a)$ is then admissible only when
$c(a)\leq b_t$, with $b_{t+1}=b_t-c(a)$.
This does not make a general expected-cost CMDP constraint equivalent
to a statewise filter, but illustrates how some history-dependent constraints
can become Markovian through state augmentation. Consequently, comparisons
between cumulative-cost and admissible-action formulations should not rely on
a blanket expressiveness claim.

\section{Intent-Constrained Markov Decision Processes}
\label{sec:icmdp}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Model and Feasibility}
\label{subsec:model}

\begin{definition}[ICMDP]
\label{def:icmdp}
An \emph{Intent-Constrained Markov Decision Process} is a tuple
\[
  \cM_I=(\cS,\cA,\cA_0,\cP,R,\gamma,I),
\]
where $(\cS,\cA,\cP,R,\gamma)$ has the usual MDP components,
\[
  \cA_0:\cS\to 2^{\cA},
  \qquad
  I:\cS\to 2^{\cA},
\]
$\cA_0(s)$ is the set of actions operationally available in state $s$, and
$I(s)$ is the set permitted by the active compiled intent rule. Transition
and reward semantics are required for the baseline-available pairs
$(s,a)$ with $a\in\cA_0(s)$; if they are defined on all of
$\cS\times\cA$, their values outside that domain are operationally irrelevant.
If every action in the global vocabulary is operationally meaningful in
every state, then $\cA_0(s)=\cA$. The compiled rule $I$ restricts admissibility;
it does not select or optimize among the remaining actions. Reward optimization
operates only over the effective admissible set defined below.
\end{definition}

User intent modifies the admissible action set associated with a state;
it does not ordinarily alter the MDP state space. State augmentation is a
separate modeling operation used when additional history or context---such as
incident or deployment status
or current intent---must be included in the Markov state.

The \emph{effective admissible set} is
\begin{equation}
  \cA_I(s)\coloneqq\cA_0(s)\cap I(s).
  \label{eq:feasible_set}
\end{equation}
The ICMDP imposes a \emph{hard admissibility condition} on every policy:
\begin{equation}
  \pi(a \given s) = 0 \quad
  \forall\, a \notin \cA_I(s),\;
  \forall\, s \in \cS.
  \label{eq:hard_constraint}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:hard_constraint} imposes admissibility at action-selection
time rather than through a cost representation.

\begin{example}[Cloud-operations running example]
\label{ex:cloud}
Consider an autonomous agent managing a production service. A simplified state
records incident severity, service health, CPU utilization, latency, replica
count, and deployment status:
\[
 s=(\text{severity},\text{health},\text{CPU},\text{latency},
    \text{replicas},\text{deployment status},\ldots).
\]
Let the global action vocabulary be
\[
 \cA=\{\operatorname{Deploy},\operatorname{Rollback},\operatorname{Restart},
 \operatorname{ScaleOut},\operatorname{ScaleIn},\operatorname{DoNothing}\},
\]
and let $\cA_0(s)$ contain the actions operationally available in state $s$.
For the instruction \emph{``Do not deploy while a Severity-1 production incident
is active,''} define the stationary compiled rule
\begin{equation}
 I_{\mathrm{sev1}}(s)
 =\{a\in\cA:\neg(\operatorname{sev1}(s)\land
 a=\operatorname{Deploy})\}.
 \label{eq:cloud_intent}
\end{equation}
The effective admissible set is
$\cA_{I_{\mathrm{sev1}}}(s)=\cA_0(s)\cap
I_{\mathrm{sev1}}(s)$. If deployment is operationally available, then
$\operatorname{Deploy}\in\cA_{I_{\mathrm{sev1}}}(s_{\mathrm{normal}})$ but
$\operatorname{Deploy}\notin\cA_{I_{\mathrm{sev1}}}(s_{\mathrm{Sev1}})$.
Reward may optimize service availability, latency, recovery time, and
infrastructure cost among the remaining actions.
\end{example}

The baseline policy class and the intent-admissible policy class are,
respectively,
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_0 \coloneqq
  \left\{
  \pi:\cS\to\Delta(\cA)
  \;\middle|\;
  \pi(a\given s)=0\ \ \forall a\notin\cA_0(s),\ \forall s\in\cS
  \right\},
  \label{eq:baseline_policy_class}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_I \coloneqq
  \left\{
  \pi:\cS\to\Delta(\cA)
  \;\middle|\;
  \pi(a \given s)=0\ \ \forall\,a\notin
  \cA_I(s),\ \forall\,s\in\cS
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:policy_class}
\end{equation}

In the formal model, $\pi$ denotes the effective action-selection
policy after admissibility enforcement. An upstream proposal policy or model
may assign probability to excluded actions, but such probability is outside
the executed policy represented by $\pi$. The admissibility function
determines which actions may be considered; the MDP objective still determines
which admissible action is preferred.

A sufficient precondition for globally well-defined Bellman updates is
global intent feasibility:
there must exist at least one admissible action in
every state.


\begin{definition}[Global Intent Feasibility]
\label{def:feasibility}
An ICMDP $\cM_I$ is \emph{globally
intent-feasible} if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \cA_I(s) \neq \emptyset \quad \forall\, s \in \cS.
  \label{eq:feasibility}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This global condition is stronger than feasibility restricted to states
reachable from a particular initial-state distribution $\rho$, but it ensures
that the Bellman operator is well defined over the full state space.
Reachability-restricted feasibility is discussed separately where relevant.

\begin{remark}[Unreachable states]
An empty admissible set at a state unreachable from the specified
initial distribution does not affect executions originating from that
distribution, although it violates the stronger global condition in
Definition~\ref{def:feasibility}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Simple sufficient condition]
If there exists an action $a_{\mathrm{safe}}$ such that
$a_{\mathrm{safe}}\in\cA_0(s)\cap I(s)$ for every $s\in\cS$, then the ICMDP is
globally intent-feasible. For example, $\operatorname{DoNothing}$ provides this
condition only when it is operationally available in every state and is never
prohibited; neither property is assumed in the general model.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Forward Feasibility]
\label{def:forward_feasibility}
A state $s$ is \emph{forward-feasible} under compiled rule $I$ if there exists
a policy supported on $\cA_I$ such that, starting from $s$, every subsequently
reached state $s_t$ satisfies $\cA_I(s_t)\neq\emptyset$ almost surely. The
condition can be checked exactly only when a transition model and a tractable
reachability or viability method are available; otherwise the runtime monitor
establishes current-state nonemptiness but not the existence of a feasible
continuation \citep{yu2022reachability}.
\end{definition}

Under global intent feasibility, nonempty continuation is immediate at the
level considered here. Forward feasibility is operationally relevant when
global feasibility cannot be certified and analysis is limited to the
reachable region or the current runtime state.

If feasibility fails at some state $s^*$, then $\cA_I(s^*) = \emptyset$
and the Bellman recursion~\eqref{eq:icmdp_bellman} below is undefined there.
At such a state, an implementation must stop, invoke a predefined safe
fallback, or request a revised instruction. Silent relaxation would change
the specified problem; any relaxation must be authorized separately.
If a safe fallback is modeled as an action within the MDP, it must
belong to $\cA_0(s)$ and satisfy the applicable intent constraints. Otherwise,
termination, human escalation, or clarification is an external control action
that ends or suspends the modeled decision process.

Unless stated otherwise, the agent observes either $s$ or an exact sufficient
statistic for both control and admissibility, and $I(s)$ is assumed to match
the user's instruction. Sections~\ref{sec:llm} and~\ref{sec:discussion}
examine these assumptions.

The distinction between fixed state-dependent admissibility and dynamic intent
is formalized in Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}.

\subsection{Optimality and CMDP Relationship}
\label{subsec:optimality}

The optimal ICMDP value function satisfies the \emph{intent-constrained
Bellman optimality equation}:
\begin{equation}
  V_I^*(s) = \max_{a \in \cA_I(s)}\!\left[R(s,a) + \gamma
    \sum_{s' \in \cS} \cP(s' \given s, a)\, V_I^*(s')\right],
  \label{eq:icmdp_bellman}
\end{equation}
with an optimal deterministic admissible decision rule selected as
\begin{equation}
  \mu_I^*(s) \in \argmax_{a \in \cA_I(s)}\!\left[R(s,a) + \gamma
    \sum_{s' \in \cS} \cP(s' \given s, a)\, V_I^*(s')\right].
  \label{eq:icmdp_optpol}
\end{equation}
The induced policy is
$\pi_I^*(a\given s)=\mathbf{1}\{a=\mu_I^*(s)\}$.


\begin{proposition}[Inherited Bellman contraction for fixed admissibility]
\label{thm:contraction}
For a finite, globally
intent-feasible ICMDP with bounded rewards and
$\gamma\in[0,1)$, define
\[
  (T_I V)(s) \coloneqq
  \max_{a\in\cA_I(s)}
  \left[R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in\cS}
  \cP(s'\given s,a)V(s')\right].
\]
Then $T_I$ is a $\gamma$-contraction under the sup norm. Consequently it has
a unique fixed point $V_I^*$, value iteration converges to that fixed point
from any bounded initialization, and a maximizing action in every state
defines an optimal stationary deterministic policy in $\Pi_I$.
These are standard consequences of discounted dynamic programming with
fixed state-dependent action sets; the proposition records the property needed
by the framework rather than asserting a novel contraction theorem.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For bounded $V,W$, the standard maximum inequality gives
\[
 |(T_I V)(s)-(T_I W)(s)|
 \leq \gamma\max_{a\in\cA_I(s)}
 \sum_{s'}\cP(s'\given s,a)|V(s')-W(s')|
 \leq\gamma\lVert V-W\rVert_\infty .
\]
Taking the maximum over $s$ proves contraction. The remaining statements follow from
the usual fixed-point and Bellman optimality arguments, since every admissible
set is finite and nonempty.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Reduction to MDP]
When $\cA_0(s)=I(s)=\cA$ for
all $s$, the ICMDP reduces to a standard MDP.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Zero-budget CMDP encoding]
\label{prop:cmdp_encoding}
Fix an initial-state distribution $\rho$, assume $\gamma\in(0,1)$, and restrict
policies to the baseline class $\Pi_0$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:baseline_policy_class}. Define the nonnegative
intent-violation cost on the baseline action process by
\[
 C_I(s,a)\coloneqq
 \mathbf{1}\{a\in\cA_0(s),\ a\notin I(s)\}.
\]
Restricting the comparison to $\Pi_0$ isolates additional exclusions introduced
by user intent from actions already unavailable under $\cA_0$. Thus $C_I$
encodes only intent exclusions on the baseline action process. We
impose the CMDP constraint
\[
 \E_{\rho,\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}
 \gamma^t C_I(s_t,a_t)\right]\leq 0.
\]
Every policy in $\Pi_I$ is feasible for this CMDP. Conversely, every policy
$\pi\in\Pi_0$ that is CMDP-feasible selects a baseline-available but
intent-excluded action with probability zero at every time step under the
trajectory distribution induced by $\rho$ and $\pi$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $\pi\in\Pi_I$, the violation cost is zero almost surely at every step, so
the expected discounted sum is zero. Conversely, every term in the discounted
sum is nonnegative. By Tonelli's theorem,
\[
  0 \geq
  \E_{\rho,\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t\geq0}\gamma^tC_I(s_t,a_t)\right]
  =\sum_{t\geq0}\gamma^t\E_{\rho,\pi}[C_I(s_t,a_t)]
  \geq 0.
\]
Because $\gamma^t>0$, each expectation is zero; nonnegativity then implies
$C_I(s_t,a_t)=0$ almost surely for every $t$, proving trajectory-level
compliance from $\rho$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Occupancy-relative support]
Let $\pi\in\Pi_0$ satisfy the zero-budget constraint. At every state $s$ with
positive occupancy at some time under the trajectory distribution induced by
$(\rho,\pi)$, the support of $\pi(\cdot\given s)$ is contained in $\cA_I(s)$.
The conclusion is trajectory-relative: it does not imply the global condition
$\pi\in\Pi_I$ at states unreachable from $\rho$.
\end{remark}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cmdp_encoding} shows that, relative to the same baseline
availability process, statewise intent exclusions admit a CMDP encoding along
reachable trajectories. ICMDP instead exposes the admissible set directly for
planning and runtime filtering; history-dependent budgets still require state
augmentation or a separate constraint mechanism.

\subsection{Dynamic Intent}
\label{subsec:extensions}

Standard MDPs assume stationary rewards and transitions. Three cases
must be distinguished. First, a fixed compiled rule $I$ may yield different
admissible sets as the environment state changes; this is ordinary stationary,
state-dependent admissibility. Second, the user's specification may change
mid-execution:
\begin{equation}
  I_t(s) \neq I_{t'}(s) \quad \text{for some } t \neq t'.
  \label{eq:dynamic_intent}
\end{equation}

Here \emph{exogenous} means that the update is supplied from outside the
modeled controlled state transition---for example, by a new user instruction---
rather than generated by $\cP$. An exogenous sequence $\{I_t\}$ generally
makes the control problem non-stationary. Third, if the active intent and its
evolution can be included in a time-homogeneous Markov state, the problem can
instead be formulated as a larger stationary MDP.

An activated intent update replaces the previously active effective action set
$\cA_{I_{\mathrm{old}}}(s)$ with
$\cA_{I_{\mathrm{new}}}(s)=\cA_0(s)\cap I_{\mathrm{new}}(s)$; it does not, by
itself, change $R$ or $\cP$. For initial intent activation, the prior action
domain may simply be $\cA_0(s)$. Three latency measures are relevant.
\emph{Interpretation/activation latency}, abbreviated below as
\emph{activation latency}, is the
interval from receipt of a potentially admissibility-changing instruction to
validated rule activation. \emph{Enforcement latency} is the interval from
validated activation to the first action-selection boundary governed by the
new rule. \emph{Optimization latency} is the interval from validated activation
until a revised constraint-aware proposal policy becomes available. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \text{instruction-to-enforcement latency}
  = \text{activation latency} + \text{enforcement latency}.
  \label{eq:latency_decomposition}
\end{equation}
During the optimization interval, an older or generic proposal policy
may be used only behind the newly activated filter: its executed actions are
compliant with the compiled intent, but its behavior need not be optimal under
that intent.

In the running example, the initial rule is
$I_1=I_{\mathrm{sev1}}$: deployment is excluded only while a Sev1 incident
is active. If the operator later instructs, \emph{``Freeze all production
deployments until further notice,''} the newly compiled rule is
\begin{equation}
  I_2(s)=\{a\in\cA:a\neq\operatorname{Deploy}\}
  \quad\forall s\in\cS.
  \label{eq:deployment_freeze}
\end{equation}
This is a genuine update $I_1\rightarrow I_2$, unlike evaluating the
unchanged $I_1$ in normal and Sev1 states. After validation and atomic
activation, $I_2$ can govern the runtime filter at the next action-selection
boundary. A policy optimized under $I_1$ may continue only behind that filter
and need not be optimal under $I_2$; planning or adaptation may therefore
compute or approximate $\pi_{I_2}^*$.

A related design conditions a policy on an intent representation $z$,
writing $\pi(a\mid s,z)$. If $z$ is part of the Markov state and its transition
law is modeled, this is an augmented-state formulation. If $z$ changes
externally, the policy may adapt without full replanning, but a separate
compiled filter remains necessary for a hard exclusion guarantee. We do not
analyze the learning or regret properties of these alternatives here.

\subsection{Intent Lifecycle}

specification timescale; the validated result is compiled into an executable
predicate and incorporated into planning on a medium timescale; and the active
predicate is evaluated at every action-selection boundary on a fast enforcement
timescale. The parser and validation stages are therefore outside the agent's
fast control loop.

\subsection{Structured Intent and Compilation}
\label{subsec:parsing}

Let $l \in \cL$ denote a natural-language instruction and $x\in\cX$ a
grounding context that identifies the action vocabulary, state fields, field
types, enumerated values, and unit conversions. Let $\cG$ denote the
structured intent language and $\cG_x^{\mathrm{val}}$ the specifications
that have been typed and grounded under $x$. Define the space of
state-evaluable intent rules as
\[
  \cR
  \coloneqq
  \{J\mid J:\cS\to 2^{\cA}\}.
\]

\begin{definition}[Intent parsing, validation, and compilation]
An \emph{LLM-based intent parser}, deterministic validator, and deterministic
\emph{intent compiler} are mappings
\begin{equation}
  f_\phi : \cL \times \cX \to \cG,
  \qquad
  v_x:\cG\to\cG_x^{\mathrm{val}}\cup\{\bot\},
  \qquad
  c : \cG_x^{\mathrm{val}}\to\cR.
  \label{eq:llm_map}
\end{equation}
For an accepted instruction,
\begin{equation}
  g_{l,x}=v_x\!\left(f_\phi(l,x)\right)\neq\bot,
  \qquad
  I_{l,x}=c(g_{l,x}).
  \label{eq:validated_compilation}
\end{equation}
Here $\phi$ denotes the parser parameters. Parsing, validation, and compilation
occur once per accepted specification; planning and runtime enforcement then
evaluate the reusable function $I_{l,x}(s)$ at hypothetical or observed
states.
\end{definition}

The core language is deliberately small. A validated specification is a finite
set of prohibitions,
\begin{align}
  g &::= \{r_1,\ldots,r_m\},
  & r &::= \operatorname{prohibit}(a,\varphi), \nonumber\\
  \varphi &::= q \mid \neg\varphi \mid
  (\varphi\wedge\varphi) \mid (\varphi\vee\varphi),
  & q &::= h\ \mathsf{op}\ u,
  \label{eq:intent_grammar}
\end{align}
where $a\in\cA$, $h$ is a typed state field, $u$ is a value of the same type,
and $\mathsf{op}$ is an operator admitted for that type. Validation requires
each action and field name to resolve uniquely, checks operator and value
types, and converts quantities to declared canonical units. An unknown action,
unknown field, ambiguous grounding, missing required value, or incompatible
unit causes $v_x$ to return $\bot$; it is not interpreted as a false condition.
Validated fields must be total on the declared state representation. A missing
field at evaluation time is an enforcement failure and invokes the configured
hold or escalation behavior rather than defaulting to $\mathsf{false}$.
The parser proposes a structured interpretation, but the validator performs
authoritative grounding against the action vocabulary, field schema,
enumerated values, and unit system in $x$. Ambiguous aliases are rejected; the
LLM is not permitted to resolve them silently.

For a validated specification
$g=\{\operatorname{prohibit}(a_j,\varphi_j)\}_{j=1}^m$, Boolean conditions have
their ordinary compositional semantics and the compiler is defined by
\begin{equation}
  c(g)(s)
  = \cA\setminus
    \{a_j:\operatorname{eval}_x(\varphi_j,s)=\mathsf{true}\}.
  \label{eq:compiler_semantics}
\end{equation}
Duplicate or overlapping prohibitions have set-union semantics and therefore
require no priority rule. The core fragment contains no positive permission
that could override a prohibition; an extension with requirements or explicit
permissions must declare conflict resolution before validation can succeed.
Thus compilation produces a general rule, not a mask tied to the state observed
during parsing. Its intersection with $\cA_0(s)$ defines the effective
admissible set.

The core language is memoryless and state-local. A historical or temporal
restriction such as ``do not deploy within 30 minutes of a rollback'' or
``allow at most three restarts per hour'' requires the relevant history to be
represented in the Markov state---for example, by
$\operatorname{time\_since\_rollback}$ or a rolling restart count---or a future
extension of the specification language with temporal operators. Temporal
syntax is outside the fragment considered here.

For the cloud instruction in Example~\ref{ex:cloud}, validation may produce
the grounded specification
\[
 g_{l,x}=\{\operatorname{prohibit}(\operatorname{Deploy},
   \operatorname{severity}=\mathrm{Sev1})\}.
\]
The language, compilation, and evaluation stages then satisfy
\begin{equation}
 v_x(f_\phi(l,x))=g_{l,x},
  \qquad
 I_{\mathrm{sev1}}=c(g_{l,x}),
  \qquad
 \cA_{I_{\mathrm{sev1}}}(s)
 =\cA_0(s)\cap I_{\mathrm{sev1}}(s).
 \label{eq:cloud_compiler}
\end{equation}
The artifact $g_{l,x}$ denotes the general predicate in
Equation~\eqref{eq:cloud_intent}, not a list of actions permitted only in the
state observed during parsing. The compiled rule can therefore be evaluated
in a current state or in hypothetical future states considered by a
planner. Reward optimization then trades service availability, latency,
recovery time, and infrastructure cost among actions in
$\cA_{I_{\mathrm{sev1}}}(s)$.

The parser $f_\phi$ performs semantic interpretation, not action optimization.
The intermediate language $\cG$ permits schema and consistency checks
before the deterministic compiler $c$ produces an executable rule. The ICMDP
optimization~\eqref{eq:icmdp_bellman} then operates over the effective
admissible set. Thus the LLM does not enforce the constraint; the compiler
produces $I(\cdot)$, while a deterministic evaluator and action filter enforce
$\cA_I(s)$. The parser and compiler can also be revised independently. No LLM call is
required inside a Bellman backup or at each decision step. The composition and
assumptions of the trusted enforcement boundary are specified in
Section~\ref{subsec:enforcement}.

\subsection{Validation and Runtime Enforcement}
\label{subsec:enforcement}

Because $f_\phi$ is learned rather than specified by a fixed grammar, it is
subject to misinterpretation. Relative to the user's intended specification,
errors can occur in two directions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Constraint over-tightening.} $f_\phi$ excludes actions the
  user did not intend to exclude, shrinking $\cA_I(s)$ and increasing the
  risk that $\cA_I(s) = \emptyset$ for some reachable $s$.
  \item \textbf{Constraint under-specification.} $f_\phi$ fails to exclude
  actions the user intended to forbid, so $I(s)$ is too permissive and the
  claim of zero executed-policy mass on actions excluded by the active compiled
  specification no longer holds with
  respect to the user's actual intent, even though it still holds with respect
  to the (incorrect) compiled rule $I(s)$.
\end{itemize}
These are failures of \emph{specification correctness}: whether the
compiled predicate expresses what the user meant. They are distinct from
\emph{enforcement correctness}: whether the system faithfully applies the
active compiled predicate to every executed action. Runtime filtering can
establish the latter relative to a compiled rule, but cannot establish the
former without additional evidence about interpretation and grounding.
Either error can change the policy class abruptly. The enforcement pipeline
therefore requires at least: (i) syntax, type,
grounding, and consistency checks on the extracted specification before it is
compiled; (ii) forward-feasibility checking under
Definition~\ref{def:forward_feasibility} when a transition model and tractable
reachability method are available,
combined with a runtime monitor that checks
$\cA_I(s_t) \neq \emptyset$ at the current state and triggers safe fallback
or clarification behavior from Section~\ref{subsec:model} when it fails; and
(iii) an audit trace recording,
for each executed action $a_t$, the effective admissible set $\cA_I(s_t)$ it was
drawn from, so that compliance with the extracted intent can be verified
after the fact. The trace records membership in $\cA_I(s_t)$, not a
statistical property of the policy. It can therefore support an audit of the
compiled filter, but it does not show that the parsed specification matches
the user's meaning.

Raw natural-language input and LLM parser output are untrusted. The trusted
boundary consists of the grounding and schema data, deterministic validator
and compiler, active-rule store, relevant trusted state source, and runtime
gate. Parser output is accepted only through the typed language; arbitrary
model-generated code is not executed. Type and schema validation can reject
malformed, ambiguous, or out-of-schema output, but cannot prove that a valid
specification captures the user's intended meaning. Compromised grounding or
state information likewise invalidates the enforcement guarantee. The
guarantee is therefore relative to the validated compiled rule and trusted
grounding and state inputs.

Natural-language instructions and grounding context may be adversarially
manipulated, including through prompt-injection-like inputs. Keeping the LLM
outside the trusted enforcement boundary limits its output to candidate
specifications that must satisfy deterministic schema, typing, and grounding
checks. These checks do not establish semantic equivalence to the user's true
intent; a malicious or incorrect interpretation may still be syntactically
valid.

\subsection{Dynamic Intent Update Protocol}
\label{subsec:update_protocol}

Section~\ref{subsec:extensions} distinguishes the mathematical cases for
changing intent. Receipt of an instruction that may alter admissibility
suspends autonomous action selection while the candidate is parsed and
validated. The previously activated rule remains stored and unchanged for
rollback and audit, but it is not treated as evidence of compliance with the
newly received instruction. The candidate is compiled under a new version and
checked for current-state nonemptiness and, when tractable, forward feasibility
under Definition~\ref{def:forward_feasibility}. If validation and these checks
succeed, the new rule is activated atomically. If validation fails or remains
ambiguous, the system remains in hold, clarification, or escalation; the
candidate is not partially installed. A less conservative continuity policy is
application-specific and cannot claim hard compliance with an unparsed new
instruction.
Once activation succeeds, the runtime filter applies the
new rule at the next action-selection boundary. Policy recomputation may then
proceed asynchronously. This ordering separates immediate enforcement of the
compiled update from the longer task of recovering optimal behavior under the
new effective admissible set: activation latency ends when the new rule is
installed, enforcement latency ends at the next governed action-selection
boundary, and optimization latency ends when the revised proposal policy is
available. In the cloud example, the deployment-freeze rule
$I_2$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:deployment_freeze} replaces the conditional Sev1
rule $I_1$ only after this activation step; subsequent actions are filtered
under $I_2$ while policy adaptation proceeds.

\section{Solution Methods and Analysis}
\label{sec:algorithms}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

ICMDP is compatible with standard restricted-action solution methods.
Constraint-aware planning applies the compiled predicate throughout policy evaluation,
improvement, Bellman backups, and learning targets: admissibility is evaluated
at both current and hypothetical successor states. A final runtime filter
independently enforces the active rule on the action actually executed.

\subsection{Intent-Filtered Planning and Learning}
\label{subsec:planning}


Planning and enforcement are complementary in the cloud example.
Suppose an action at $s_0$ can lead to a future Sev1 state $s_1$. An
unconstrained planner may value that action while assuming that
$\operatorname{Deploy}$ will remain available at $s_1$. The runtime filter
prevents deployment after $s_1$ is reached, but it does not correct the earlier
valuation at $s_0$. Constraint-aware planning instead computes
$V_I^*(s_0)$ using the future effective admissible set
$\cA_I(s_1)$ and can prefer a trajectory with a better admissible
continuation. Runtime filtering guarantees membership of the executed action;
it does not by itself produce a policy optimal for the restricted problem.

\begin{example}[Why runtime filtering alone is insufficient]
\label{ex:runtime_only}
Let $\gamma=0.9$. At $s_0$, action $\operatorname{Safe}$ yields reward $5$ and
terminates, while $\operatorname{Risky}$ yields reward $0$ and transitions to a
Sev1 state $s_1$. At $s_1$, $\operatorname{Deploy}$ yields reward $10$ and
terminates but is prohibited; $\operatorname{Recover}$ is admissible, yields
reward $1$, and terminates. An unconstrained planner computes
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{unconstrained}}(s_0,\operatorname{Risky})
  =0.9(10)=9>5
\]
and therefore selects $\operatorname{Risky}$. If a runtime-only gate masks
$\operatorname{Deploy}$ and reselects from the remaining admissible actions,
$\operatorname{Recover}$ is the only admissible continuation, producing return
$0.9(1)=0.9$. By contrast,
a constraint-aware planner uses
\[
  V_I(s_1)=1,
  \qquad
  Q_I(s_0,\operatorname{Risky})=0.9<5,
\]
and selects $\operatorname{Safe}$. Planner-visible admissibility and final
runtime enforcement are therefore complementary: the gate ensures local
compliance, while the planner avoids upstream valuations that rely on
prohibited future actions.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Value iteration.}
Value iteration restricts the Bellman update~\eqref{eq:icmdp_bellman}:
\begin{equation}
  V_{k+1}(s) = \max_{a \in \cA_I(s)}\left[R(s,a) + \gamma
  \sum_{s' \in \cS} \cP(s' \given s, a)\, V_k(s')\right].
  \label{eq:intent_vk}
\end{equation}
The restriction to $\cA_I(s)$ is a state-dependent action constraint applied
before the Bellman backup \citep{huang2022,lin2021actionconstrained}.

\paragraph{Policy iteration.}
Policy evaluation is unchanged but applied to an admissible policy
$\pi \in \Pi_I$. Define
\[
  Q^\pi(s,a)\coloneqq R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in\cS}
  \cP(s'\given s,a)V^\pi(s').
\]
Policy improvement selects a deterministic admissible decision rule,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{k+1}(s) \in \argmax_{a \in \cA_I(s)} Q^{\pi_k}(s,a)
  \quad \forall\, s \in \cS.
\end{equation}
The induced policy places unit mass on $\mu_{k+1}(s)$. For a directly specified
admissible set, no Lagrangian relaxation is required; compliance with the
effective admissible set follows by construction
of $\Pi_I$.

\paragraph{Model-free learning.}
In model-free settings, the effective admissible set $\cA_I(s)$ can be enforced
through action masking in Q-learning and policy-gradient algorithms
\citep{huang2022,lin2021actionconstrained}.
Both the behavior policy used for exploration and
the next-state bootstrap must be restricted to the admissible sets. For a
sampled admissible action, the update is
\begin{equation}
  Q(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t) + \alpha\!\left[r_t
    + \gamma \max_{a' \in \cA_I(s_{t+1})} Q(s_{t+1},a')
    - Q(s_t,a_t)\right],
  \quad a_t \in \cA_I(s_t),
\end{equation}
where $r_t$ is the realized one-step reward sample. For policy-gradient
methods, masked logits $z_\theta(s,a)$ yield
\begin{equation}
  \pi_\theta(a \given s) =
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{\exp\bigl(z_\theta(s,a)\bigr)}
          {\displaystyle\sum_{a' \in \cA_I(s)} \exp\bigl(z_\theta(s,a')\bigr)}
    & \text{if } a \in \cA_I(s), \\[8pt]
    0 & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:masked_policy}
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to setting inadmissible logits to $-\infty$ before the
softmax and gives them zero executed-policy probability \citep{huang2022}. Thus the
masked distribution is the executed or effective policy. Sampling for
exploration uses the current-state mask, the bootstrap target uses the
successor-state mask, and policy-gradient log-probability and entropy terms are
computed from the masked distribution during training as well as execution.

Action masking preserves the standard restricted-action formulation;
Proposition~\ref{thm:contraction} establishes convergence only for finite-model
value iteration. No additional convergence guarantee is claimed here for
Q-learning or policy-gradient methods under function approximation.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Failure Modes}
\label{subsec:analysis}

\paragraph{Convergence and complexity.}
Proposition~\ref{thm:contraction} gives convergence of
\eqref{eq:intent_vk}. With an
explicit transition model, the per-iteration backup cost is
\[
  O\!\left(\sum_{s\in\cS}\sum_{a\in\cA_I(s)}
  |\operatorname{supp}\cP(\cdot\given s,a)|\right),
\]
plus the cost of evaluating or retrieving each admissible set. Small admissible
sets reduce Bellman-backup work. The costs occur on three timescales: language
parsing, validation, and compilation on the slow specification timescale;
policy computation or adaptation on the medium planning timescale; and
deterministic predicate evaluation, filtering, and logging on the fast
enforcement timescale. Because the LLM is outside the fast loop, its latency
affects instruction updates rather than every Bellman backup or action.

\paragraph{Empty admissible sets.}
If operational availability or compiled intent leaves
$\cA_I(s) = \emptyset$ for some reachable
$s$, then~\eqref{eq:icmdp_bellman} is undefined there (Definition
\ref{def:feasibility}). Any implementation must therefore include a
feasibility monitor that checks $\cA_I(s_t) \neq \emptyset$ before action
selection and triggers a predefined safe fallback, termination, or user
clarification when it fails, as described in Section~\ref{subsec:model}.
When only local runtime feasibility is available rather than the global
condition of Definition~\ref{def:feasibility}, current-state nonemptiness does
not by itself establish a feasible continuation.

\paragraph{Changing constraints.}
Dynamic intent is non-stationary when the rule changes exogenously
(Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}). The activation protocol in
Section~\ref{subsec:update_protocol} enforces a validated replacement at the
next action-selection boundary while planning adapts asynchronously. The
resulting activation, enforcement, and optimization latencies are those defined
in Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}; in particular, enforcement need not wait
for optimization.

\paragraph{Parser errors.}
The specification and enforcement failure modes are analyzed in
Section~\ref{subsec:enforcement}; downstream planning cannot correct a parser
that has produced the wrong validated rule.

\subsection{Planned Evaluation}
\label{subsec:planned_evaluation}

A future reproducible simulated cloud-control ICMDP evaluation will compare
(i) unconstrained planning, (ii) finite reward penalties, (iii) a zero-budget
CMDP using the same compiled intent exclusion, (iv) runtime filtering only,
(v) constraint-aware planning without the final runtime gate, and (vi) combined
constraint-aware planning and runtime enforcement. The evaluation will report
policy return, action-level violation rate, infeasibility frequency, exact
specification or predicate accuracy where ground truth is available, parser
over- and under-restriction rates, clarification frequency, activation latency,
enforcement latency, instruction-to-enforcement latency, optimization latency,
runtime-filter overhead, and audit completeness. It will
include clean, ambiguous, and deliberately perturbed instructions and the
mid-execution deployment-freeze update. No empirical results are claimed here.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{subsec:guarantees}

Given correctly specified $\cA_0$ and $I$, every policy in $\Pi_I$ assigns
zero executed-policy probability to actions excluded by the active compiled
specification, by construction
(Section~\ref{subsec:model}), not by statistical estimation. This statement
depends on four assumptions from
Section~\ref{subsec:model}: the true state (or an exact sufficient statistic)
is observed, $\cA_0$ correctly represents operational availability,
$I$ is derived correctly from the user's instruction, and the
ICMDP is globally intent-feasible.

For exogenous dynamic intent, the same statement is version-relative:
after a validated rule $I_t$ is atomically activated, the executed policy
assigns zero executed-policy probability to actions excluded by the active
compiled specification---that is, actions outside
$\cA_{I_t}(s)$---at subsequent
action-selection boundaries. This version-relative claim assumes correct activation, state
information, predicate evaluation, and filtering. It neither applies
retroactively nor implies that a policy awaiting reoptimization is optimal for
the revised effective admissible set.

Each assumption may fail in practice:

\paragraph{Parsing errors.}
Section~\ref{subsec:enforcement} distinguishes over- and under-specification
and explains why enforcement of a compiled rule does not establish semantic
agreement with user intent.

\paragraph{Partial observability.} The present formulation assumes state
observability. A belief-dependent
admissibility function $I(b)$---conditioning intent on a belief state
rather than the true state---is left for future work.

\paragraph{Scalability.} The pipeline invokes an LLM when a new instruction or
changed grounding requires parsing or re-validation
(Section~\ref{subsec:extensions}). Model latency can delay such updates;
caching and pre-compilation reduce repeated work but cannot eliminate the cost
of interpreting a genuinely new instruction.

\paragraph{Availability during updates.}
The conservative hold-on-update protocol trades operational availability for
assurance. A system requiring uninterrupted control may need an
application-specific continuity policy, with correspondingly weaker claims
about immediate compliance with an unresolved instruction.

\paragraph{Absence of empirical evaluation.}
The paper contains no quantitative comparison of policy performance, empirical
solver study, or evaluation of noisy intent extraction. The formal results
hold under their stated assumptions but do not establish performance in a
deployed system. The cloud-operations scenario in Example~\ref{ex:cloud} is
explanatory and has not been evaluated in a production control plane.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper develops ICMDP as a formal interface between natural-language
instructions and action admissibility in sequential decision making. Baseline
operational availability is separated from user-imposed restriction, and their
intersection defines the effective admissible set used by both planning and
execution.
The resulting model retains the standard discounted-MDP properties under fixed
admissibility, while Proposition~\ref{prop:cmdp_encoding} locates its precise
relationship to a zero-budget CMDP representation.

The central contribution is a validated and versioned
language-to-admissibility lifecycle that connects semantic interpretation to
control. Natural language is mapped to a validated typed specification; a
deterministic compiler establishes an executable rule; the same rule is visible
to planning over hypothetical states and enforced by the final runtime gate.
The LLM remains outside the trusted boundary. Atomic version activation
separates enforcement latency from optimization latency, allowing the new rule
to govern execution before a proposal policy has been re-optimized. This
separation also makes the guarantee and its boundary explicit: enforcement can
provide hard exclusion under the active compiled rule, while specification
correctness remains a distinct validation problem. Together, these elements
provide a coherent basis for agents whose linguistic instructions must
constrain both prospective reasoning and final action.
